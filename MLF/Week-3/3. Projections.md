- Want to project b onto the line trough x, or, more generally onto the column space of a matrix A.
## Why project? 
- Given a basis for a subspace S (e.g. spanned by cols of A) is there an easy to calculate the projection P of b onto S?
	- Suppose we are given $(x_1, b_1) \dots (x_n, (b_n))$
	- e.g. $2x = b_1 \text(or) x+2y = 4$
	- "These are inconsistent" $=>$ No solution that satifies this system of equations
## Matrix view : 
- $Ax = b$
- Inconsistent if $b \in C(A)$
- In such situations it makes sense to proejct b onto C(A)
## Projections onto a line
- $p = \hat{x}a$
- $e = b-p = b- \hat{x}a$
- $e \bot a$
- $(b-\hat{x}a) \bot a$
- $a^T(b-\hat{x}a) = 0$ leading to $\hat{x} = \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}a$
## Cauchy - Schwarz inequality
- $||e||^2 = ||b-p||^2 \geq 0$
- $||b - \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}a||^2 = b^Tb - \frac{2(a^Tb)}{a^Ta} + ({\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}})^2 a^Ta$
- $\frac{(b^Tb) (a^Ta) - (a^Tb)^2}{a^Ta} \geq 0$
- or $|a^T b| \leq ||a|| ||b||$
## Projection Matrix
- Recall $p =\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}a = (\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}b)$
- Let $\mathbb{P} = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}$ then, projection of b onto a is $\mathbb{P}$ b
- To project any vector b, just left multiply by the projection matrix '$\mathbb{P}$'
### Example : 
- $a = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\1\\1 \end{bmatrix}$
Projection matrix is $\mathbb{P} = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta} = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1& 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix}$
- Observe that 
	1. $\mathbb{P}$ is symmetric
	2. $\mathbb{P}^2 = \mathbb{P} \text{ i.e. } \mathbb{P}^b = \mathbb{P}b$ 
		1. Idea : $\mathbb{P}b$ is already on the line through $a$. So, another road of projection won't change it.
	3. Column space($C(\mathbb{P})$) = line through a = $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\1 \end{bmatrix}$
	4. Null space $N(\mathbb{P})$ = Plane orthogonal to a = $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\1 \end{bmatrix}$
		1. Rank $r(\mathbb{P}) = 1$
	5. a = $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \mathbb{P} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} & \frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix}$
