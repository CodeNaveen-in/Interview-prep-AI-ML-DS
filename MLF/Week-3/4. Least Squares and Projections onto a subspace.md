# Back to least squares
- $\text{This system is solvable } \begin{cases} 2x=b_1 \\ 3x=b_2 \\ 4x=b_3\end{cases} \text{ if b is on the line through }\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$
- Suppose we have a vector "$b$" that leads to an "inconsistent" system.
- We could pick a subset of equations & solve it exactly. 
	- Problem with this approach : large errors in some inputs & no error in others.
	- Reasonable alternative : minimize average error
	$$E^2 = (2x-b_1)^2 + (3x-b_2)^2 + (4x-b_3)^2$$
	- Want to minimise the sum of squares $E^2$
	$$\frac{\partial E^2}{\partial x} = 0 <=> 2 [2(2x-b_1) + 3(3x-b_2) + 4(4x-b_3)] = 0$$
	- Leading to $\hat{x} = \frac{2b_1 + 3b_2 + 4b_3}{2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2} = \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}$ with a = $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$
	- Bottomline : Taking derivative & finding the minima turns out to be the same as performing a projection.
## Projection onto a subspace
	$Ax = b , A \text{ is } m \times n, m> n$
- Want projection of b onto column space C(A
- Projection of b onto S is $p = A \hat{x}$
- Orthogonal vector $e = b-p = b - A \hat{x}$
- Q : How to find $\hat{x}$ ?
	- Observe $e \bot$ every vector in $C(A)$
	- Recall that $C(A) \bot N(A^T), \text{ i.e. }, N(A^T)$ is the orthogonal complement of $C(A)$, i.e. every vector in $C(A)$ is orthogonal to every vector in $N(A^T)$ & any given vector is in either $C(A)$ or $N(A^T)$
- Where does $e$ belong? 
	- $e \in N(A^T) => A^Te = 0 => A^T(b-A\hat{x}) = 0$
	- leading to $A^TA\hat{x}  = A^Tb$
		- Equation to solve to obtain the projection of b onto $C(A)$
		- (Note : Even if $Ax=b$ is not solvable, $A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$ has a solution)
	- Alternative route to the above equation : 
		- A = $\begin{bmatrix} | & | & \dots & |\\ a_1& a_2& \dots& a_n \\| & | & \dots & | \end{bmatrix}$
		- $\begin{cases} a_1^Te = 0  \\ \vdots \\ a_n^Te = 0\end{cases} <=> \begin{cases} a_1^T(b-A^A_x) = 0 \\ \vdots \\ a_n^T(b-A_x^A)= 0 \end{cases}$
		- $\begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ \vdots \\ a_n^T \end{bmatrix} [b-A\hat{x}] = 0 <=> A^T(b-A\hat{x} = 0)$ leading to $A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$
	- Bottomline : Solving $A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$ leads to an $\hat(x)$ that minimizes $||Ax-b||^2$
	- connection of projections to least squares
## Remarks
1. Suppose columns of A are linearly independent C(i)
	1. Then, $A^TA$ is invertible (Why?)
	2. Solving $A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$ when $(A^TA)$ is invertible
		1. $\hat{x} = (A^TA)^{-1} A^Tb$
		2. Projection $\mathbb{P} = A\hat{x} = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb$
2. $b \in C(A) \text{ i.e. } b = Ax$
	1. $p = A(A^TA)^{A^Tb} = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^TAx = Ax = b$
3. $b \in N(A^T)$
	1. $p = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb = 0$ since $A^Tb = 0$
4. A is square & invertible <=> $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$
	1. $p = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb = AA^{-1}(A^T)A^Tb = b$
5. A is rank one i.e. , A = $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ a \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
	1. Then, $\hat{x} = \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}$
	2. Coincidence with what we dervied earlier for the projection onto a line
# Projection Matrix
- $P = A(A^TA)^{-1} A^T$
	- Symmetric $\mathbb{P}^T = \mathbb{P}$
	- $\mathbb{P}^2 = \mathbb{P}$
- So the projection matrix is symmetric & satisfies $\mathbb{P}^2 = \mathbb{P}$
- The converse is also true.
- Claim : 
	- If $\mathbb{P}^2 = \mathbb{P}$ & P is symmetric, then $\mathbb{P}$ is a projection matrix.
	- $\mathbb{P}b =$ projection of b onto the column space of $\mathbb{P}$
	- $(b-\mathbb{P}b)^T\mathbb{P}c = b^T (I - \mathbb{P}^T)\mathbb{P}c = b^T(\mathbb{P} - \mathbb{P}^2)c = 0 \text{ since } \mathbb{P}^2 = \mathbb{P}$
